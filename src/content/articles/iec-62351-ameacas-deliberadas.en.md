When we think of a deliberate threat against an OT environment, the first image is usually that of a hacker trying to compromise a system over the network.

IEC 62351-1 shows that the picture is much broader.

In section **5.2.3 — Deliberate threats**, the standard presents different intentional threats capable of affecting electrical systems:

- dissatisfied employees;
- industrial espionage;
- vandalism;
- hackers;
- viruses and worms;
- theft;
- terrorism.

The common point between them is the existence of intention.

The attacker seeks to gain some advantage, access something without authorization, cause damage or compromise an asset.

## The attacker may be inside the organization

One of the first scenarios presented by IEC 62351 is the dissatisfied employee.

This threat deserves special attention because an insider may possess knowledge that an external attacker does not have.

They may know:

- system architecture;
- equipment;
- processes;
- procedures;
- location of critical assets;
- forms of access;
- limitations of existing controls.

In electrical systems, where certain technologies are extremely specific, this knowledge can make an insider attack particularly dangerous.

Cybersecurity, therefore, cannot be thought of exclusively as protection against external threats.

## Information also has value

The standard also presents industrial espionage as a threat.

In this case, the objective may not be to stop the operation.

The attacker may be interested in:

- technical information;
- operational knowledge;
- strategic data;
- intellectual property;
- information about competitors.

This is important because OT environments are often associated primarily with availability.

But **confidentiality is also relevant**.

A project, a configuration or certain knowledge about the installation can have high economic and strategic value.

## Vandalism can be physical or logical

Another threat presented is vandalism.

The attacker may simply wish to cause harm, regardless of whether he or she obtains any financial benefit.

This damage can occur physically, but it can also involve actions on systems and applications.

Shutting down equipment or a software application can be as intentional as physically damaging an asset.

IEC 62351 once again reinforces the importance of monitoring and identifying anomalies.

## Cyber ​​hackers and the myth of the isolated system

The section on hackers brings perhaps one of the most relevant reflections of the standard.

There is a tendency to see cybersecurity as:

> “protect the system against someone coming from the Internet”.

Following this reasoning, if the OT system is isolated from the Internet, it would be secure.

IEC 62351 directly questions this perception.

Operating environments may need different forms of access, for example:

- remote access from suppliers;
- maintenance laptops;
- access for engineers;
- connections used to collect information;
- other interconnections between systems.

> **Isolation reduces certain risks, but does not eliminate the need for cybersecurity controls.**

This is an extremely current concept for OT environments.

## Malware can also penetrate isolation

The same logic appears when the standard addresses viruses and worms.

Even a system without a direct connection to the Internet can receive software or files from another environment.

A compromised maintenance laptop can act as a vector.

Contaminated software can be installed on an isolated system.

Other connected equipment may introduce the threat.

This means that risk analysis needs to consider not only the permanent connections of the system, but also **how that environment is maintained, updated and accessed throughout its lifecycle**.

## Theft doesn't just mean equipment

IEC 62351 uses a very interesting definition of theft.

What can be taken?

- equipment;
- data;
- knowledge.

This perspective brings physical security and information security closer together again.

Copying information without authorization and physically removing equipment are different events, but both represent improper access to an asset.

## Terrorism and systemic impact

Finally, the standard presents terrorism as a threat of lower probability, but with an extremely high potential impact.

There is another important concept here.

The greatest damage does not necessarily occur through the physical destruction of an entire facility.

An attacker who understands how the system works can look for a specific asset whose unavailability produces greater consequences for the electrical system.

This reinforces the need to understand:

- criticality of assets;
- dependencies;
- consequences of the loss of a certain function;
- impact on the system as a whole.

## Cybersecurity is not just a firewall

Section 5.2.3 leaves an important message.

- **The attacker** can be inside or outside the organization.
- **The attack** can be physical or digital.
- **Motivation** can be financial, political, competitive or simply destructive.
- **The target** can be equipment, software, information or knowledge.

Therefore, reducing OT cybersecurity to the existence of a firewall between the industrial network and the Internet is a limited vision.

Protecting electrical systems requires understanding:

> **Who can pose a threat, what has value, which paths can be used and what consequences may arise if the attack is successful.**

This is the broader view of deliberate threat presented by IEC 62351.
