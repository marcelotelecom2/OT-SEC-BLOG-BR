## Standard summary

*Editorial summary of IEC 62351-1. Highlighted passages are not presented as verbatim quotations; the normative document remains the reference.*

When we think about cybersecurity, it is common to associate threats with hackers, malware or network attacks.

**IEC 62351-1**, however, presents a broader vision.

In section **5.2 — Types of security threats**, the standard considers as assets not only equipment, hardware and facilities, but also people, information, databases and software applications.

Therefore, protection must consider both **physical threats** and **cyber threats**.

### Not every threat is deliberate

Section **5.2.1 — General** makes an important distinction: a threat can arise from both an unintentional event and a deliberate attack.

The standard itself highlights that failures related to safety, equipment failures, human carelessness and natural disasters can cause significant impacts on electrical systems.

This greatly expands the way of thinking about cybersecurity in OT.

It's not enough to just ask:

**“Who can attack the system?”**

It is also necessary to consider:

**“What can compromise the system even without an attacker?”**

The standard also reinforces the importance of **risk analysis** to determine what needs to be protected, against which threats and with what level of security.

And there is another important principle: **there is no single solution for all scenarios**. Layers of security are preferable to relying on a single protection.

### 5.2.2 — Inadvertent threats

Next, IEC 62351 presents four types of **unintentional threats**.

#### Safety failures

Safety has always been a central concern in the electrical sector, especially in high voltage environments.

The standard highlights that procedures remain fundamental, but that monitoring, records and alarms can help reinforce them.

**Author’s application comment:** An interesting point is that some controls initially created for safety reasons can also contribute to security.

Access control at a substation, for example, can protect people and, at the same time, help reduce vandalism and theft.

#### Equipment failures

Equipment failures are treated as expected threats to the reliable operation of the electrical system.

Monitoring conditions such as temperature, cooling, voltage, frequency or overload is already part of the operational reality.

IEC 62351 draws attention to the fact that this information can also help in other aspects, such as maintenance, fault detection and subsequent analysis of events.

In other words, monitoring created for operation can also bring security benefits.

#### Carelessness

The human factor also appears as a threat.

The standard cites examples such as:

- allow *tailgating*;
- leave doors unlocked;
- allow improper access to passwords or keys.

The problem is not always in a malicious action.

It can arise from complacency, carelessness or even the perception that a certain safety control hinders work.

**Author’s application comment:** This is an especially important point in OT: **a control is only really effective if it also works within operational reality**.

#### Natural disasters

Storms, hurricanes and earthquakes are also included in the analysis.

These events can cause electrical system failures, safety problems and create opportunities for vandalism, theft or other threats.

Therefore, the standard highlights the value of real-time monitoring of the physical and informational state of facilities and equipment.

This monitoring works as the **“eyes and ears”** of the operation during adverse situations.

## Technical analysis

*The author’s technical reading of the summarized concepts; it does not establish additional normative requirements.*

The message of this first part of section 5.2 is simple:

> **threat does not necessarily mean attacker.**

From an information security and OT cybersecurity perspective, we also need to consider human error, equipment failure, safety issues and natural events.

IEC 62351 thus begins by building a vision of security that goes beyond cyber attacks and considers the electrical system as a set of **people, equipment, information, processes and operational environment**.

In the next part, we will enter the other group presented by the standard: **5.2.3 — Deliberate threats**, deliberate threats.
