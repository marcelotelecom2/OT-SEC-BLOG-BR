After presenting unintentional threats, IEC 62351-1 addresses in section **5.2.3 — Deliberate threats** the scenarios in which there is intentional action against electrical system assets.

These are very different threats:

- dissatisfied employees;
- industrial espionage;
- vandalism;
- hackers;
- viruses and worms;
- theft;
- terrorism.

The common point is the existence of an intention to obtain some advantage, cause damage or compromise an asset.

## Attacks can be targeted

Unlike an accidental failure, a deliberate threat can specifically choose the asset it intends to compromise.

An attacker who knows the installation's architecture can, for example, look for equipment whose unavailability causes a much greater operational impact than simply attacking a random component.

Therefore, IEC 62351 highlights the importance of monitoring, real-time alarms and the existence of trails that allow the incident to be subsequently investigated.

## Disgruntled employee

The dissatisfied employee appears as one of the important threats to electrical systems.

There is a clear reason for this.

An insider may know:

- architecture;
- equipment;
- processes;
- credentials;
- operational procedures;
- critical points of the installation.

In highly specialized environments, such as the protection and control of electrical systems, this knowledge can make an internal attack significantly more effective than an attempt carried out by someone completely external.

Internal risk, therefore, should not be ignored.

## industrial espionage

Information is also an asset.

Technical data, designs, configurations, operational strategies and system knowledge can have great economic value.

Industrial espionage seeks precisely to obtain this type of information without authorization.

IEC 62351 also recognizes that the objective may go beyond financial gain. Information can be used to harm a competitor's image, reliability or position.

This reinforces the importance of confidentiality even in environments traditionally more concerned with availability.

## Vandalism

Vandalism can have an extremely simple motivation: causing damage.

The attacker may not even fully understand the consequences of his action.

In a substation, this can mean anything from physical damage to equipment to the intentional shutdown of systems or applications.

The standard once again highlights monitoring as a relevant mechanism for identifying abnormal access or behavior.

## cyber hackers

The section on hackers brings one of the most interesting reflections on IEC 62351.

There is a tendency to associate cybersecurity exclusively with protection against hackers from the Internet.

The reality of OT environments is more complex.

Even when operating systems do not have a direct connection to the Internet, there are other paths that can introduce risks:

- remote access from suppliers;
- maintenance laptops;
- engineering stations;
- expert access for data collection;
- interconnections with other systems.

That's why:

**isolation should not be confused with security.**

An isolated architecture can reduce certain attack surfaces, but it does not eliminate the need for cybersecurity controls.

## Viruses and worms

The same principle appears when the standard addresses malware.

A virus or worm does not necessarily have to arrive directly from the Internet.

Software installed on a workstation, a compromised laptop, or other equipment connected to the environment can act as an entry vector.

This concept remains extremely current in industrial environments.

Protecting an OT system needs to consider not only its permanent connections, but also the equipment and processes used for maintenance, upgrades and engineering.

## Theft

IEC 62351 uses an interesting definition of theft.

The stolen object could be:

**equipment, data or knowledge.**

This again demonstrates the integration between physical security and information security.

Physically removing equipment and copying confidential information are different actions, but both represent the loss of an asset for which the attacker did not have authorization.

## Terrorism

Finally, the standard addresses terrorism as a threat of lower probability, but with the potential for extremely high consequences.

Another important point is that a sophisticated attack does not necessarily need to destroy an entire installation.

Compromising specific critical equipment can, depending on the architecture and function performed by the asset, cause much greater systemic impacts.

This observation reinforces the need to understand criticality and dependencies within the electrical infrastructure.

## Cybersecurity needs to look beyond the Internet

Section 5.2.3 of IEC 62351 leaves an important message for those working with OT:

**the attacker does not have just one way to reach the system.**

It can be internal or external.

The attack can be physical or digital.

The objective may be financial, political, competitive or simply to cause harm.

And the asset sought can be equipment, an application, information or even operational knowledge.

Therefore, protecting electrical systems requires more than installing a firewall between the OT network and the Internet.

It is necessary to understand **who can attack, which assets have value, which paths can be used and what consequences may arise if the attack is successful.**

This broad threat view is one of the bases for understanding the security approach presented by the IEC 62351 series.
